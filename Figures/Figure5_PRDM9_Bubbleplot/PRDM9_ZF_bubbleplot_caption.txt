PRDM9 zinc-finger array size heterogeneity across non-human primates.

A modernized version of Schwartz et al. (2014)'s own Figure 3 — same underlying idea
(bubble size encodes how many independently sequenced alleles land at a given array
size), but combining four sources rather than one, and grouped by clade (Apes, orange;
Old World Monkeys, purple; New World Monkeys, teal; Tarsiers, pink; Lemurs, gold — the
same color scheme as Figures 1-4 for the first four, plus a 5th color for the one clade
outside this review's usual four-clade scheme, shown once in the legend above) rather
than Schwartz's own ad hoc per-species/pooled-genus categories. Clades are ordered
Apes/Old World Monkeys/New World Monkeys/Tarsiers/Lemurs to match phylogenetic
placement; species are ordered by clade, then by descending mean array size within
each clade block. Point shape indicates whether the species has any fine-scale
recombination-rate (RR) data in this review at all: filled circle = fine-scale RR data
exists (the species also appears in this review's Gini survey, Figure 2 Panel B); open
triangle = PRDM9 has been characterized but no fine-scale recombination map exists for
the species anywhere in this review or, as far as this review's literature search
found, in the published field. Bubble size is the number of independently sequenced
alleles observed at that exact array size (legend lower right); species with only one
or two sequenced alleles are necessarily small, single dots and should be read with
that uncertainty in mind, not as a settled species average the way Pan troglodytes'
34-allele distribution can be.

Twenty-five species/genera are shown, spanning four data sources: (1) Schwartz et al.
(2014)'s own raw per-allele sequences (Supplementary Data 1, not their summary figure,
which pools New World and Old World monkey genera into unresolved categories) —
providing this figure's entire chimpanzee (n=34 alleles), bonobo (n=4), marmoset (n=1),
and a second independent gorilla sample, plus three species listed under taxonomic
synonyms not obviously searchable from their current names: Nomascus leucogenys and
Nomascus gabriellae appear as "Hylobates leucogenys" and "Hylobates gabriellae" (the
crested gibbons were later split into their own genus), and Lagothrix lagotricha
appears as "Simia lagotricha"; (2) an unpublished allele set generously sequenced by
L. Ségurel and M. Przeworski (real PCR-cloned, Sanger-sequenced alleles from named
gorilla, orangutan, siamang, and rhesus macaque individuals, using the same wet-lab
methodology as Schwartz et al. — available via the Przeworski lab website but, as far
as this review found, not previously plotted alongside the published primate PRDM9
literature); (3) new tBLASTn/GeneWise/Princeton-ZF-tool annotation work done directly
against each species' own genome assembly for three species entirely absent from both
published sources — Chlorocebus sabaeus (vervet), Papio anubis (olive baboon;
recovered from a locus mislabeled as the PRDM7 paralog in the reference annotation),
and Daubentonia madagascariensis (aye-aye); and (4) Heerschop et al. (2016)'s own
Table 1, a direct per-individual allele survey (28 alleles, 23 individuals, 15
heterozygous) spanning five named Tarsius species (T. syrichta, T. bancanus, T.
dentatus, T. lariang, T. fuscus) plus several unclassified individuals affiliated with
the T. tarsier population complex, contributing the entire Tarsiers block.

Three findings stand out. First, this review's zero-recombination-data species are not
a minor footnote: orangutan (both Pongo abelii and Pongo pygmaeus) and siamang
(Symphalangus syndactylus) — each with real, substantial PRDM9 array-size data (up to
6 sequenced alleles per species) — have no fine-scale recombination map, no cM/Mb
estimate, and no Gini value anywhere in this review or, as far as this review's search
found, in the literature at all; the same is true for every non-Nomascus gibbon
species, Macaca nemestrina, Nasalis larvatus, Papio cynocephalus, and Lagothrix
lagotricha shown here — and now for every tarsier taxon as well, an entire clade with
real PRDM9 data and zero fine-scale recombination data anywhere in the field. Second,
Plecturocebus cupreus (titi monkey) — despite having a published fine-scale
recombination map elsewhere in this review — is the one species in this review where
PRDM9 itself could not be characterized at all (no genome annotation, no recoverable
GeneWise gene model, no allele-level sequencing in any source checked), shown as an
empty labeled gap between Callithrix jacchus and the Tarsiers block rather than
omitted from the axis, to make the absence visible rather than silent. Third, Tarsius
syrichta's single sequenced allele (3 zinc fingers) is the shortest PRDM9 array
anywhere in this figure — shorter than any other species, including the two species
already flagged elsewhere in this review as unusually short-armed (Chlorocebus
sabaeus and Daubentonia madagascariensis, both 7) — though this rests on a single
sequenced allele from a single individual and should be read as suggestive, not
definitive, pending a larger tarsier sample. Full per-allele data and source
attribution in supplemental_zf_alleles_table.csv.

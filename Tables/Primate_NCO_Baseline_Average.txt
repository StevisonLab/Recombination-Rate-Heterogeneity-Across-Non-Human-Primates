PRIMATE NON-CROSSOVER (NCO) RATE BASELINE AVERAGE
Companion note to Primate_cMMb_Baseline_Average.txt
Computed from Master_Comparison_Table.xlsx

PURPOSE

Same exercise as the cM/Mb baseline note, applied to the non-crossover (NCO)
rate: report the central tendency and spread across every primate species in
this review with a genuine per-bp-per-generation NCO rate, so a reader has a
sense of what "typical" looks like and how much to trust any single
species' number in isolation. Unlike cM/Mb, there is no commonly-cited
"default" NCO rate in general use to test against here -- this note is about
characterizing the spread itself, not checking it against a folk assumption.
Standalone text for the discussion, not a new figure -- Figure 1 (NCO rate
ring), Figure 3 (tract length + CO:NCO fraction deep dive), and Figure 4
(sex-specific NCO rate ratio) already cover this metric visually; this is
the numeric summary to go with them.

SPECIES AND UNITS

All 9 species in this review with a genuine, directly comparable NCO rate (of
18 species total with any recombination data), all already in the same unit
(events per bp per generation, x10^-6) -- no unit-conversion or Ne-dependent
calibration issue the way cM/Mb had, since these are direct per-generation
event rates, not population-scaled historical estimates. No exclusions
needed on unit grounds. One number per species (sex-averaged/pooled where a
species has separate male/female rates -- see Finding 3 for where that
hides real structure).

RESULT -- THE AVERAGE

  n = 9 species
  mean   = 3.32 x10^-6 per bp per generation
  median = 2.65 x10^-6
  SD     = 2.01 x10^-6
  range  = 0.68 - 7.52 x10^-6  (an 11-fold spread)

Ranked highest to lowest, with method tagged:

  7.52  Papio anubis (olive baboon)              [Pedigree -- Wall et al. 2022]
  5.00  Papio papio (Guinea baboon)               [Long-read, n=1 individual]
  3.95  Plecturocebus cupreus (titi monkey)        [Pedigree -- Versoza et al. 2026]
  3.48  Hylobates lar (lar gibbon)                 [Long-read, n=1 individual]
  2.65  Macaca nemestrina (pig-tailed macaque)     [Long-read, n=1 individual]
  2.54  Gorilla gorilla gorilla (western gorilla)  [Long-read, n=3 individuals]
  2.18  Macaca mulatta (rhesus, Indian-origin)     [Pedigree, paternal-only by design]
  1.86  Pan troglodytes verus (western chimp)      [Long-read, n=4 individuals]
  0.68  Daubentonia madagascariensis (aye-aye)     [Pedigree -- Versoza et al. 2025]

An 11-fold range on a 9-species sample is a much wider relative spread than
cM/Mb showed (2.6-fold across 14 species) -- expect NCO rate to be a
substantially noisier metric to generalize from, both because the true
biological variation may be larger and because (see Finding 1) two
structurally different measurement approaches are pooled into this one
ranked list.

FINDING 1 -- TWO METHODS, NOT DIRECTLY COMPARABLE DESPITE MATCHING UNITS

The single most important caveat: the 9 species split evenly into two
structurally different kinds of measurement that happen to share units.

  Pedigree-based (4 species: olive baboon, rhesus macaque, titi monkey,
  aye-aye) -- direct event counts from parent-offspring trios, divided by a
  stated phase-informative-site denominator and number of meioses. A raw,
  empirical count over a known number of transmissions.

  Porsborg et al. (2025) sperm/testis long-read (5 species: Guinea baboon,
  lar gibbon, pig-tailed macaque, gorilla, chimp) -- a MODEL-BASED rate, fit
  from gene-conversion events detected in pooled germ cells from testis
  tissue, correcting for detectability, tract length, and postmeiotic-cell
  fraction. Not a raw count divided by a denominator -- a statistical
  model's output.

Splitting the 9-species set by method:
  Pedigree (n=4):  mean 3.58 x10^-6, median 3.07, SD 2.95, range 0.68-7.52
  Porsborg (n=5):  mean 3.11 x10^-6, median 2.65, SD 1.21, range 1.86-5.00

The two subgroup means are similar (3.6 vs 3.1), which is reassuring at the
coarsest level, but the pedigree group's SD is more than double the Porsborg
group's (2.95 vs 1.21) -- almost entirely because the pedigree group contains
both the single highest value in the whole set (baboon, 7.52) and the single
lowest (aye-aye, 0.68), while the Porsborg values cluster more tightly
(1.86-5.00). Whether that's a real difference in measurement precision
between the two methods, true biological variation concentrated in the 2
outlier pedigree species, or both, isn't resolvable from this data alone --
flag it as an open question rather than asserting either explanation.

Do not read cross-method rank differences (e.g., "why is Papio anubis so
much higher than Papio papio, a different species in the same genus,
measured by the other method") as a clean biological signal without this
caveat attached -- long-read haplotype-switch detection and pedigree
transmission calling plausibly differ in NCO tract-length sensitivity
independent of any true species difference.

FINDING 2 -- SEVERAL VALUES ARE SINGLE-INDIVIDUAL POINT ESTIMATES

3 of the 5 Porsborg species (Guinea baboon, lar gibbon, pig-tailed macaque)
are each a single sequenced individual -- point estimates with no
within-species replication and no error bar in this review's table. Gorilla
(3 individuals) and chimp (4 individuals) are the only Porsborg species with
any between-individual spread to gauge precision from (chimp ranges
1.26-2.36 x10^-6 across its 4 individuals, gorilla 1.95-3.08 across its 3 --
i.e., even the "well-replicated" Porsborg species show close to 2-fold
individual-to-individual variation, which should temper confidence in the
single-individual species' point estimates too). The pedigree species are
better powered in this specific sense (built from multiple offspring/trios,
not one individual) but still rest on modest absolute event counts (85 NCO
events for macaque, 184 for titi monkey, 200 for aye-aye, 263 tracts for
baboon) -- not large-sample estimates by absolute standards either, just
less exposed to single-individual idiosyncrasy than 3 of the 5 Porsborg
values are.

FINDING 3 -- SEX-SPECIFIC STRUCTURE IS HIDDEN IN THE POOLED NUMBERS ABOVE

3 of the 4 pedigree species have separate, and sometimes quite different,
male/female rates that the sex-averaged figures above obscure:
  Papio anubis:                  maternal 9.71, paternal 5.34   (x10^-6)
  Plecturocebus cupreus:         maternal 4.20, paternal 3.69
  Daubentonia madagascariensis:  maternal 6.4 (x10^-7), paternal 7.4 (x10^-7)
                                  -- i.e. the ONE species in this set where
                                  the male rate exceeds the female rate

Macaca mulatta is paternal-only by the study's own design (7 paternal
meioses, no maternal data at all) -- its 2.18 figure is not a sex-averaged
value the way the other 3 pedigree species' are, and is not directly
comparable to them on that basis alone. The 5 Porsborg species are
inherently male-only (testis tissue), so "sex-averaged" doesn't apply to
them either -- every Porsborg value in this note is implicitly a male rate.
Combined with the note above, only 3 of the 9 species (baboon, titi monkey,
aye-aye) have a genuine two-sex sex-averaged rate; the other 6 are
single-sex (5 obligately male via tissue type, 1 male-only by pedigree
design) presented on equal footing in the ranked list. See Figure 4
(heterochiasmy) for the sex-ratio angle specifically -- Daubentonia's
male-biased NCO rate is flagged there as notably running opposite its own
female-biased crossover-rate heterochiasmy, a genuine finding worth carrying
into the text.

WHAT'S NOT IN THIS NOTE

- The other two NCO-adjacent metrics this review already computed -- mean
  NCO tract length and NCO fraction of all detected recombination events
  (NCO / (NCO+CO)) -- are deliberately not repeated here; see Figure 3's
  supplemental table for those, including the caveat (from the aye-aye
  paper's own authors) that cross-species tract-length comparison is
  "challenging" due to differing phase-informative marker density (a
  primary-literature caveat, not just a review-added one).
- 9 species with no NCO data at all (Papio hamadryas, Nomascus leucogenys,
  Pan paniscus, Chlorocebus sabaeus, Callithrix jacchus, Alouatta caraya,
  and the 3 Sapajus/Cebus species) are excluded because their only available
  method(s) (LD-based, cytogenetic MLH1-foci) cannot structurally detect
  NCO events at all -- a method limitation, not a data-access gap.

BOTTOM LINE

Primate NCO rate in this review centers around 2.7-3.3 x10^-6 per bp per
generation (median/mean), but with far more relative spread (11-fold) than
this review's cM/Mb comparison showed, split roughly evenly across two
structurally different measurement approaches (direct pedigree counts vs.
model-based long-read estimates) that agree at the level of subgroup means
but not precision, and built substantially on single-individual or
modest-sample-size point estimates. Treat any individual species' NCO rate
in this review as a rough, method-dependent estimate rather than a precise,
directly cross-species-comparable figure -- more so than this review's
cM/Mb values, and much more so than a reader might assume from the fact that
all 9 numbers share the same units.

PRDM9-INDEPENDENT ("DEFAULT") HOTSPOT ACTIVITY vs. GINI COEFFICIENT
Does reliance on PRDM9-independent hotspots track genome-wide recombination
unevenness — and in which direction?
Companion note to Figure 2 (Gini dotplot)

BACKGROUND

Joseph, Prentout, Laverré, Tricou & Duret (2024, PNAS 121(23):e2401973121,
"High prevalence of PRDM9-independent recombination hotspots in placental
mammals") developed a GC-biased-gene-conversion (gBGC)-based estimator of
historical recombination activity at loci orthologous to mouse Prdm9-/-
knockout DSB hotspots (MDH loci — the promoter-/CpG-island-associated
"default" hotspots a genome falls back on when PRDM9 isn't directing
recombination) across 52 boreoeutherian mammals. Their central finding is
that PRDM9-directed and PRDM9-independent hotspots COEXIST in the large
majority of mammals (63%, 33/52, show a clear recombination peak at default
loci) rather than one excluding the other — only 25% (13/52, including
humans and mice) show an actual deficit at default loci.

That coexistence finding matters for how to frame the expected relationship
with Gini. A naive "substitution" framing — default hotspots taking the
place of PRDM9-directed ones — would predict that heavier reliance on the
dispersed, numerous default pathway smooths out the sharp, concentrated
PRDM9-driven peaks, producing a LOWER Gini (more even distribution). But
Joseph et al.'s own result argues against substitution as the right model
for most species: if default and PRDM9-directed hotspots typically coexist
and add to each other rather than trade off, then a species with elevated
default-hotspot activity ON TOP OF its PRDM9 activity would be expected to
show MORE total concentration of recombination at hotspots overall — a
HIGHER Gini, not a lower one. Under this additive/compounding framing, a
positive correlation between default-hotspot activity and Gini is the more
mechanistically sensible expectation, not a contradiction of it.

DATA

Joseph et al.'s per-species supplementary data (their Dataset S1/S2/S3)
includes three species that exactly match this review's own Gini panel
(Callithrix jacchus, Pan troglodytes, Chlorocebus sabaeus) plus one close
congener (Macaca fascicularis standing in for this review's Macaca mulatta
-- the same kind of congener substitution used elsewhere in this review).
Their `relrechot_KO` metric (relative recombination rate at MDH loci,
underlying their Figure 3) is correlated here against this review's own
LDhat-based Gini values, chosen specifically to avoid the pyrho-smoothing
estimator confound described in the companion zinc-finger note.

  Species                 relrechot_KO (Joseph et al. 2024)  Gini (this review, LDhat)
  Callithrix jacchus       1.102                              0.8068
  Macaca fascicularis*     1.193                              0.7944 (M. mulatta, Xue et al. 2020)
  Pan troglodytes          1.210                              0.8210 (Stevison et al. 2016)
  Chlorocebus sabaeus      1.422                              0.9523 (Pfeifer 2020)

  * congener standing in for Macaca mulatta

RESULT

  All 4 species:                     Pearson r = 0.936  (n=4, p=0.064)
  3 exact species matches only:      Pearson r = 0.969  (n=3, p=0.159)
    (dropping the Macaca fascicularis congener)

Both correlations are positive and consistent in direction, whether or not
the congener substitution is included. Neither reaches conventional
significance at this small n, but as discussed above, the positive
direction itself is the expected sign once the relationship between the two
hotspot types is framed as additive (coexisting, compounding concentration)
rather than substitutive (trading off toward evenness) — the framing Joseph
et al.'s own genome-wide survey supports for most mammals.

Chlorocebus sabaeus (vervet) drives the pattern: it has both the highest
relrechot_KO and the highest Gini of the four species, and is also this
review's single most concentrated (highest-Gini) genome overall. Under the
additive framing, this is a coherent picture — vervet's fine-scale
recombination landscape may be shaped by unusually strong default-hotspot
activity layered on top of its PRDM9-directed activity, rather than by
either mechanism alone.

INTERPRETATION

Of the two PRDM9-related mechanisms tested in this chapter (the other being
zinc-finger array size, see the companion note), this one offers a more
plausible mechanistic account of the cross-species Gini pattern observed
here, even though it does not reach statistical significance at n=4. A
substitution-based version of this hypothesis would have been contradicted
by these results; the additive/coexistence version Joseph et al.'s own data
supports is not — it is consistent with the direction seen here, just not
yet confirmed at this sample size. Extending this test to more species, and
ideally computing `relrechot_KO`-equivalent estimates directly for more of
this review's own Gini panel rather than relying on species overlap with
Joseph et al.'s own sampling, is a clear next step (see the chapter's
discussion of future directions).

CAVEATS

- Very small sample (n=4, or n=3 exact matches). This result should be read
  as consistent with the additive framing, not as confirmation of it —
  a larger, purpose-built comparison across more species with both a
  computable Gini and a directly-estimated relrechot_KO (or equivalent)
  would be needed to treat this as a settled finding.
- The macaque data point relies on a congener substitution (Macaca
  fascicularis standing in for Macaca mulatta) rather than a same-species
  match, consistent with this review's general congener-substitution
  convention but still a source of uncertainty specific to this
  comparison.
- Joseph et al.'s taxonomic sampling of primates is limited relative to
  this review's own panel — only 3 exact species overlaps exist between
  the two datasets, which is why this test cannot currently be extended to
  more of this review's Gini species without new analysis.

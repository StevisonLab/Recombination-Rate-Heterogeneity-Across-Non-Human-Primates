PRDM9-INDEPENDENT ("DEFAULT") HOTSPOT ACTIVITY vs. GINI COEFFICIENT — HYPOTHESIS TEST
Does heavier reliance on PRDM9-independent hotspots predict a more even
(lower Gini) genome-wide recombination distribution?
Companion note to Figure 2 (Gini dotplot)

HYPOTHESIS

Joseph, Prentout, Laverré, Tricou & Duret (2024, PNAS 121(23):e2401973121,
"High prevalence of PRDM9-independent recombination hotspots in placental
mammals") developed a GC-biased-gene-conversion (gBGC)-based estimator of
historical recombination activity at loci orthologous to mouse Prdm9-/-
knockout DSB hotspots (MDH loci — the promoter-/CpG-island-associated
"default" hotspots a genome falls back on when PRDM9 isn't directing
recombination) across 52 boreoeutherian mammals. Their central finding:
PRDM9-directed and PRDM9-independent hotspots coexist in the large majority
of mammals (63%, 33/52, show a clear recombination peak at default loci)
rather than one excluding the other — only 25% (13/52, including humans and
mice) show an actual deficit.

This raises a candidate mechanism for cross-species Gini variation distinct
from PRDM9 zinc-finger array size (see the companion note,
PRDM9_ZF_vs_Gini_Hypothesis_Test.txt): if a species relies more heavily on
the numerous, dispersed default pathway relative to the sparse, sharp
PRDM9-directed pathway, its genome-wide recombination distribution might
plausibly read as more even (lower Gini).

DATA

Joseph et al.'s per-species supplementary data (their Dataset S1/S2/S3)
includes three species that exactly match this review's own Gini panel
(Callithrix jacchus, Pan troglodytes, Chlorocebus sabaeus) plus one close
congener (Macaca fascicularis standing in for this review's Macaca mulatta
-- the same kind of congener substitution used elsewhere in this review).
Their `relrechot_KO` metric (relative recombination rate at MDH loci,
underlying their Figure 3) is correlated here against this review's own
LDhat-based Gini values, chosen specifically to avoid the pyrho estimator
confound described in the companion zinc-finger note.

  Species                 relrechot_KO (Joseph et al. 2024)  Gini (this review, LDhat)
  Callithrix jacchus       1.102                              0.8068
  Macaca fascicularis*     1.193                              0.7944 (M. mulatta, Xue et al. 2020)
  Pan troglodytes          1.210                              0.8210 (Stevison et al. 2016)
  Chlorocebus sabaeus      1.422                              0.9523 (Pfeifer 2020)

  * congener standing in for Macaca mulatta

RESULT

  All 4 species:                     Pearson r = 0.936  (n=4, p=0.064)
  3 exact species matches only:      Pearson r = 0.969  (n=3, p=0.159)
    (dropping the Macaca fascicularis congener)

Both correlations are POSITIVE — more PRDM9-independent hotspot activity
goes with a MORE concentrated, not more even, genome-wide distribution in
this review's own data, the opposite sign from the naive version of the
hypothesis.

INTERPRETATION

Neither correlation clears conventional significance at this n, so this
cannot be read as a confirmed effect in either direction — but the direction
is consistent whether or not the congener substitution is included, and
Chlorocebus sabaeus (vervet) is the point driving the pattern: it has both
the highest relrechot_KO and the highest Gini of the four species. This is
notable because vervet is also the species that broke Hypothesis 1's
premise (see the companion zinc-finger note) — two independent PRDM9-
adjacent mechanisms, tested against two different subsets of this review's
own species, both fail to explain vervet's position as this review's single
most concentrated (highest-Gini) genome. Whatever is driving vervet's Gini,
it does not appear to be either of the two PRDM9-linked mechanisms currently
proposed in the literature, consistent with Pfeifer (2020)'s own statement
that the cause is "a question in need of further investigation."

A SUGGESTIVE BUT UNUSABLE DATA POINT — LEMUR CATTA vs. AYE-AYE

Joseph et al.'s panel includes no species from this review's own Gini set
for Lemuriformes, but it does include Lemur catta, and the result is
striking on its own terms: Lemur catta has the single highest default-
hotspot activity of any primate in their entire dataset (relrechot_KO =
2.409 -- nearly double vervet's), despite carrying a fully intact,
14-zinc-finger PRDM9 with no sign of pseudogenization. This review's own
aye-aye Gini (0.4509) is separately the LOWEST -- most even -- value in the
whole review, by a wide margin.

Those two facts point in the same qualitative direction the hypothesis
originally predicted (heavy default-hotspot reliance <-> more even
distribution) even though Lemur catta and Daubentonia madagascariensis are
different genera and this is NOT a valid same-species data point -- if
anything, it's the opposite pattern from what the four haplorhine species
above showed, meaning the sign of this relationship may not even be
consistent across clades. A direct test would require either (a) applying
Joseph et al.'s own gBGC-based estimator to Daubentonia madagascariensis
directly (their method only needs whole-genome alignments and substitution
data, not a fine-scale recombination map, so aye-aye's existing genome
assembly should in principle be usable without new sequencing), or (b) the
reverse -- computing a Gini coefficient for Lemur catta if a fine-scale map
is ever generated for it (currently only cytogenetic MLH1-based data exists
for that species in this review, which structurally cannot produce a Gini
value at all). Either direction would resolve whether Lemuriformes
genuinely reverses the sign seen in apes/Old World monkeys, or whether the
Lemur catta vs. aye-aye comparison is simply two unrelated genera that
happen to sit at opposite extremes for unrelated reasons -- flagged here as
a concrete, well-motivated direction for future work rather than resolved
with the data on hand.

CAVEATS

- Very small sample (n=4, or n=3 exact matches). Neither correlation is
  statistically significant, and a 3-4 point correlation is a fragile basis
  for any conclusion beyond "does not support the naive hypothesis as
  stated in this direction, with this data."
- The macaque data point relies on a congener substitution (Macaca
  fascicularis standing in for Macaca mulatta) rather than a same-species
  match, consistent with this review's general congener-substitution
  convention but still a source of uncertainty specific to this
  comparison.
- Joseph et al.'s taxonomic sampling of primates is limited relative to
  this review's own panel — only 3 exact species overlaps exist between
  the two datasets, which is why this test cannot currently be extended to
  more of this review's Gini species.
